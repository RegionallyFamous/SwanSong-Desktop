# The Dawn Shift

<!-- scene: scene-05 -->

Platform zero was not on any station map. Mr. Vale led them through the maintenance hatch and down three steps that should have ended inside a wall.

Instead, they emerged beside a narrow track under a roof of painted stars. Six passengers waited on benches with no advertisements: a child holding an adult's briefcase, a bride in muddy boots, a conductor whose uniform belonged to another century. None looked dead. All looked delayed.

Mara carried the Orange Platform Blend in Lio's dented service pot. Teo carried cups. That division had once meant Mara trusted herself with the important object. Now she noticed he had packed exactly seven cups, including theirs.

The passengers paid with promises written on scraps: return the library book; apologize before explaining; learn the recipe instead of preserving the card. Each promise made the brass key warmer.

The final passenger was Lio's empty red coat, buttoned around the shape of someone sitting.

Mara's breath caught. Teo did not reach for her. He set down the cups within reach and waited.

Inside the coat pocket, Mara found a voice recorder. Lio's message was brief and irritatingly cheerful. She did not ask Mara to keep the kiosk unchanged. She asked Mara to teach someone well enough that they would improve it. Then the recording clicked off before goodbye.

The absence of farewell hurt worse than a speech would have. It also left room.

Mara poured a cup for the red coat. Steam passed through the collar and clouded the painted stars. When it cleared, the coat lay flat. Beneath it waited the second train ticket and a new label for the key: SHARED TOOLS MUST SHOW THEIR WEAR.

Mara held the key toward Teo. “What do you choose?”

He answered without a joke. “A trial dawn shift. You keep the right to hate my labels.”

“I already hate them.”

“Then our governance is functioning.”

This time Mara laughed, and grief moved aside enough to let the sound through.

Morning arrived orange along the ordinary tracks. Platform zero folded back into a maintenance wall, leaving Mr. Vale's broom trapped on the mysterious side. He filed a grievance against metaphysics.

At the kiosk, Teo lettered the new blend in chalk. His handwriting leaned uphill. Mara corrected the price, resisted correcting the name, and handed him the brass key.

“The dawn shift is not ownership,” she said. “It is responsibility with an alarm clock.”

<!-- voice: foil -->
“And insufficient whimsy before six.”

Their first customer was the conductor from the last train. He accepted the salt in the Orange Platform Blend, frowned as if reviewing a timetable, and bought a second cup for the engineer.

The key changed in Teo's palm. Its newest scratch caught the window light. Mara had spent eleven months protecting Lio's things from wear, as though untouched objects could keep a person near. The scratch did not erase Lio. It proved the tool had survived into another hand.

Mara took the second train ticket from her pocket. It was valid for any destination after closing. She pinned it beside the schedule rather than hiding it in the ledger.

“Next Sunday,” she told Teo. “We close early. You choose where.”

He stared at her with none of his practiced dignity. “That sounds dangerously like a holiday.”

“Do not make me revoke it.”

The kiosk clock ticked normally. Cardamom and orange crossed the platform as the first commuter train arrived. Behind them, the flour tin clicked open by itself.

Inside lay a parcel dated tomorrow and addressed in an unfamiliar hand.

Teo reached for it, then glanced at Mara.

She nodded.

He opened it while she poured the next cup.
