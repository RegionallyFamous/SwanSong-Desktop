# The Key in the Cup

<!-- scene: scene-01 -->

<!-- voice: protagonist -->
Mara counted the brass key's teeth twice. “Objects do not become innocent because you put them in a teacup.”

Cardamom steam pressed against the locked kiosk shutter. Above platform four, the last train indicator shed one amber minute at a time. Teo stood between Mara and the ticket gate with his raincoat dripping into a perfect little island. He had the posture of a defendant and, disastrously, the hopeful eyebrows of a puppy.

“Emergency reliquary,” he said. “Not camouflage.”

The cup belonged to Mara's sister. So did the rule painted beneath its saucer: NO BORROWED KEYS AFTER SUNSET. Lio had written it after a summer of lost spoons, runaway delivery bicycles, and one memorable incident involving a mayoral sash. Lio had been dead eleven months. Her handwriting had no business looking fresh.

Teo admitted the key had opened locker 113. The parcel inside carried Mara's name in that same sharp blue script. Its paper was dry despite the leaking station roof, and it smelled faintly of cloves and machine oil.

The departure bell chimed. Mara could board, carry the impossible parcel home, and spend the night finding sensible explanations. Instead, she saw Teo watching her hands, not the train. He knew what leaving cost her. He was trying not to ask her to pay it.

Mara turned the key over. One tooth bore a dusting of orange sugar from a recipe Lio had never shared outside the family.

“Show me the locker,” she said.

The train left without them. Its rails narrowed to a silver complaint, and the kiosk clock—unplugged since the funeral—ticked once.

Emergency light flattened the locker corridor into copper and gray. Locker 113 stood open. The shelf held only a clove-colored ring and a railway claim ticket dated tomorrow.

<!-- voice: foil -->
Teo bowed to the empty compartment. “Honored passenger, we apologize for the delay and for the absence of biscuits.”

“Please do not improvise ghost etiquette.”

“Then the station should post official guidance.”

The night caretaker arrived pushing a broom that had never met a corner it respected. Mr. Vale—not related, though he treated the coincidence as a personal achievement—denied remembering the parcel. Then he corrected Teo's bow.

“Lower,” the caretaker said. “A railway guest outranks a customer after closing.”

Mara nearly laughed. The sound reached her throat and found grief standing there. She covered it by asking Mr. Vale to deny the impossible part in chronological order.

His keys rattled. He claimed the dead timetable had always covered a maintenance hatch. He claimed Lio had never bribed him with saffron buns. He claimed the little blue arrow on tomorrow's ticket pointed nowhere.

Teo crouched and aligned the ticket with the clove ring. The arrow pointed beneath the timetable.

Mr. Vale stopped sweeping.

“Your sister asked me to keep one compartment unlisted,” he said. “Until you missed a train for someone else.”

Mara wanted to dislike the manipulation. Instead she remembered every time Lio had missed the seven-ten because Mara needed help closing. The anger came cleanly then, warm enough to use.

She made Mr. Vale open the hatch. Behind it waited a narrow drawer, a red recipe ledger, and six sealed envelopes. Five bore dates from the past year. The sixth was labeled: WHEN MARA FINALLY LETS TEO CHOOSE.
